# Pepperdine Supplement: Faith Essay

**Prompt:** Pepperdine is a Christian university where all are welcomed and encouraged to challenge each other in the pursuit of truth. Students, faculty, and staff members from all backgrounds participate in conversations of faith inside and outside of the classroom. Considering that Pepperdine is a Christian university, why are you interested in attending and how would you contribute to conversations of faith on campus?

**Draft (493 words; replace the bracketed sentence with a true detail):**

"We do not compel you to accept it. You are free to make your own choice, but we want you to know what it is."

George Pepperdine was describing the Christian way of life, but he could have been describing the education I want. I'm an atheist, and I'm not applying to Pepperdine in spite of its Christian identity. In part, I'm applying because of it.

Many people I love believe in God. [My grandmother prays for me every night, and my best friend hasn't missed youth group in years.] Still, whenever our conversations drift toward faith, someone changes the subject to keep the peace. I used to think that was the polite thing to do. Now I think it's a loss, because the questions we avoid are the ones that matter most: where morality comes from, what makes a life meaningful, how to face suffering. I want to spend college somewhere those questions are asked out loud, in classrooms and dorm rooms, by people who genuinely disagree. Pepperdine affirms that truth, "having nothing to fear from investigation," should be pursued relentlessly in every discipline. I don't share the faith behind that sentence, but I agree with every word of it.

That includes investigating my own beliefs. It's easy to reject a caricature of Christianity; it's harder to engage its best thinkers alongside classmates who live their faith every day. If my convictions can't survive four years of honest conversation, they aren't worth keeping. If they can, I'll understand them, and the people who see the world differently, far better than I do now.

My first contribution would be genuine curiosity. I'd treat Chapel and my religion courses as real intellectual work, reading the Bible as the enormously influential text it is, not a box to check. I'd ask hard questions to understand, not to win arguments, and I'd listen longer than is comfortable. For Christian classmates, I can offer a perspective they may rarely hear up close: someone who takes their faith seriously without sharing it. Proverbs says iron sharpens iron, and sharpening takes a little friction. I also hope to show other non-religious students that they belong in these conversations, not on the sidelines.

Most of all, I'd look for common ground in how we live. Pepperdine's motto, "Freely ye received, freely give," describes something I believe, even though I arrive at it by a different road. I'd rather serve alongside classmates whose reasons differ from mine than debate them from a distance. Working side by side is often where the most honest conversations begin.

George Pepperdine offered students two things: a real look at the Christian way of life, and the freedom to make their own choice. I want both. I can't predict where four years of these conversations will lead. But I know I'll leave understanding faith more deeply and my own beliefs more honestly, with friendships built on exactly the conversations most people are too polite to have.
